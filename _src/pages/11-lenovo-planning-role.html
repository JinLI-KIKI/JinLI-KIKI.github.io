Scope

I owned end-to-end supply planning for Lenovo's North American notebooks in three portfolios: commercial, education and public sector, and small and medium business. That meant balancing demand, supply, inventory, capacity and risk for every product in them, from launch through end-of-life.

A planner sits between teams that all see a different part of the product. Sales knows the deals, R&D knows what is changing in the product, procurement knows the parts, and the factories know what they can build. The plan is where those views have to agree.

A month in the cycle

  1. Demand in

    Take the agreed North American demand plan by product and configuration.

  2. Plan supply

    Run MRP and supply planning in SAP IBP against material, ODM capacity and lead times.

  3. Commit

    Publish what can be built, where and when, and flag the gaps.

  4. Reconcile

    Work the gaps and risks through with sales, procurement and manufacturing, and agree the trade-offs.

  5. Execute and watch

    Track builds, shortages, excess and end-of-life transitions until the next cycle.

The North American IBP cycle as I ran it, simplified. Each step feeds the next month's plan.

SAP IBP key user

Besides planning in SAP IBP, I was one of the team's key users for it. When the way we planned needed something the system didn't do, I wrote the requirement, tested the change when it came back, and trained colleagues on how to use it.

Where the system couldn't keep up with what we needed every week, I built tools around it. The parts-to-box commit tool came out of that.

What it taught me

The plan is a negotiation

The numbers are the easy part. Most of a planning cycle is getting sales, the factories and procurement to accept the same trade-off.

Tariffs move the target

Tariffs were never one of a planner's own measures, but in 2025 they decided whether the year's shipment target could be met, because they raise what customers pay.

Every plan leaves a tail

Whatever a plan commits today can become excess later. Thinking about end-of-life at the start of a product saves most of the clean-up at the end.

Tools follow the cycle

The best automation targets the step people repeat every week by hand, not the one that looks most impressive.
