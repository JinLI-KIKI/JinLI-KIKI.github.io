Tariff pull-in for North America

After the November 2024 election it was clear tariffs on China-made goods were coming, and most of our North American notebooks were built in China. I pulled supply forward so the stock was in the US before the duty started.

The situation

On 25 November 2024 the president-elect said he would add 10% to tariffs on Chinese goods on his first day. Nothing was in force yet, and nobody knew the rate, the date or which products would be hit. What was certain was that a notebook built in China and shipped after the duty started would cost more to land in the US than the same notebook shipped before it.

The extra duty came in on 4 February 2025 and doubled to 20% on 4 March. In April, computers were exempted from the much larger reciprocal tariffs, but the 20% stayed.

What I did

  1. Read the risk early

    Between the election and the inauguration, I treated the tariff as likely rather than waiting for the details, and prepared contingency plans.

  2. Pulled future supply forward

    Moved planned North American builds earlier in the plan, so they could ship before the duty started.

  3. Built finished-goods stock for North America

    Prepared finished-goods inventory ahead of the tariff dates to protect availability and cost.

  4. Rebalanced other regions

    Adjusted other regions' supply so they could help cover what North America took early.

  5. Asked the factories for more line capacity

    Worked with the factories to expand lines, giving room to absorb the pull-in and whatever the policy did next.

The trade-off

A pull-in is a bet on timing. Both directions cost money.

What it taught me

Act on the signal, not the rule

By the time the tariff was published, the factories and freight were already busy. The useful window was the uncertain one.

Capacity is the real constraint

Pulling forward is easy to plan and hard to build. It only worked because the factories could add line capacity.

A pull-in buys time, not a fix

Stock shipped early covers a few months. Avoiding the duty for good meant changing where the product was made, which became the Vietnam transition.

One region's win costs another

Supply is global. Every unit pulled into North America early was a unit another region had to plan around.
